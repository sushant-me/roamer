36 hours. One question I couldn't stop thinking about.

Where on Earth should we test Moon and Mars hardware?

Last weekend I built AstroLens for NASA Space Apps 2026 (challenge #09). It started as a space learning app — point your phone at the night sky and it names the stars — but the part I'm proudest of is the Earth Analogs engine underneath.

Everyone names Atacama as the Mars analog. But Atacama is already one of the most studied places on Earth. So I asked a different question: where would a field survey actually teach us something new?

34 sites. One 500 m grid. Five pass/fail gates. One Survey Gap score:
gap = similarity × (1 − literature attention) × operability

The controls are my favourite part:
→ Atacama passes 5/5 but scores 0.00 — because it's already surveyed.
→ Pokhara fails aridity and scores 0.00.
→ Chhusang leads at 0.23; Kagbeni 0.16.

A 0.00 isn't a bug. It's the method working.

I also added 267 stars, 88 constellations, a gyro sky dome, a 3D solar system, and a 4.6-billion-year Story of Earth — because an app about space should make you look up, not just read a table.

What I learned:
• Controls are credibility. A method that can't fail isn't a method.
• Name your proxies. Ours is an OpenAlex count, cross-checked with Crossref (r ≈ 0.69).
• Catch your own inconsistencies. I found a threshold label that didn't match the code, fixed it, and added a regression test.
• Scope is a feature. If I had to cut one feature, it'd be the 3D solar system; the challenge answer stays.

It's not a certification — it's a shortlist for investigation, with the evidence attached.

The certificate says "Galactic Problem Solver" 🙂 — the real win was shipping a working thing and learning how much honesty a small data product needs.
